# Unlocking India's Billion-Dollar MVNO Opportunity: Why TRAI Must Lower the Entry Barrier Now

*By Saurabh Pant | Telecom Strategy & Digital Infrastructure Leader*

---

India's telecom story is one of remarkable contrasts. The country is home to the world's second-largest subscriber base, has among the cheapest mobile data tariffs globally, and recently commissioned nearly 97,500 indigenously developed 4G towers under the Atmanirbhar Bharat initiative. Yet when it comes to Mobile Virtual Network Operators (MVNOs) — the innovation layer that sits atop existing network infrastructure and has catalysed billions of dollars in economic value across Europe, the United States, and increasingly Southeast Asia — India remains conspicuously absent from the global map.

This is not an accident of market forces. It is a consequence of regulatory architecture that has kept entry barriers prohibitively high for over seventeen years, effectively locking out an entire category of innovation from one of the world's most dynamic telecom markets. The time for a decisive policy intervention is now — and the opportunity cost of further delay is measured in billions of dollars.

---

## What MVNOs Are — And Why They Matter

A Mobile Virtual Network Operator is a telecommunications provider that offers mobile services to customers without owning the radio spectrum or physical network infrastructure. Instead, an MVNO purchases wholesale network capacity from a licensed Mobile Network Operator (MNO) and resells it under its own brand, often targeting specific customer segments or use cases that the MNOs themselves are ill-suited to serve efficiently.

This model is not marginal. Globally, the MVNO market was valued at approximately USD 84.6 billion in 2023 and is projected to reach USD 116.8 billion by 2028, growing at a compound annual growth rate of 6.7%, according to MarketsandMarkets research. The IoT-specific MVNO segment — which powers connected devices across agriculture, healthcare, logistics, and smart cities — is growing even faster, with projections suggesting the market will expand from USD 4.16 billion in 2025 to nearly USD 14 billion by 2032 at a CAGR of approximately 18.9%.

The MVNO model has proven its capacity to unlock value that traditional MNOs simply cannot access efficiently. MVNOs have built sustainable businesses serving diaspora communities with preferential international rates, agricultural cooperatives with precision farming connectivity, healthcare systems with secure medical-grade data services, and enterprise clients requiring custom service level agreements. In each case, the MVNO's ability to focus on a defined customer segment — without the capital burden of network ownership — is precisely what enables the innovation.

India, with its extraordinary diversity of consumer segments, its vast agricultural economy, its rapidly digitising healthcare system, and its ambition to become a global IoT manufacturing hub, is ideally positioned to benefit from this model. The question is why it has not.

---

## Seventeen Years of Regulatory Inertia

The story of MVNO licensing in India is one of sustained regulatory hesitation. The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India first issued recommendations on MVNOs in August 2008, following a reference from the Department of Telecommunications seeking guidance on the need, timing, and framework for MVNO introduction. Those recommendations established a broadly sensible framework: a 20-year renewable licence, entry fees set at 10% of the applicable MNO entry fee for the same service area, and a net worth eligibility requirement of Rs. 10 crore for Category A circles, Rs. 5 crore for Category B, and Rs. 3 crore for Category C.

The Department of Telecommunications, however, has never issued a final policy decision implementing those recommendations. For nearly two decades, the question of MVNO licensing in India has remained in a state of administrative suspension — neither formally rejected nor operationally implemented.

The consequences of this inertia are visible in the numbers. While Europe hosts hundreds of MVNOs across its member states, and the United States has seen players ranging from Tracfone to Google Fi build substantial businesses on virtual operator models, India's MVNO ecosystem consists largely of franchise arrangements and informal reseller models that lack the regulatory standing to operate as genuine virtual operators.

A recent consultation paper by TRAI on the subject of Mobile Virtual Network Operators noted that the existing framework — including net worth requirements and entry fee structures — disadvantages MVNOs and increases barriers to entry. This acknowledgement from the regulator itself underscores the urgency of reform.

---

## The Entry Barrier Problem: A Closer Look

The current TRAI framework, as recommended in 2008 and never updated, sets MVNO entry fees at 10% of the MNO entry fee for the relevant service area. For a Category A circle — which includes the largest and most commercially valuable service areas — this translates to a maximum of Rs. 5 crore (approximately USD 600,000) as an entry fee. Add to this the net worth requirement of Rs. 10 crore and the annual licence fee equivalent to that of the host MNO, and the financial burden on a prospective MVNO becomes substantial.

For context, the Telecommunications Act of 2023 represents India's most comprehensive legislative reform of the sector in decades, and TRAI has been actively issuing recommendations on the framework for service authorisations under this new legislation. Yet the MVNO-specific framework has not been substantially reformed in parallel.

The entry fee and net worth barriers are particularly consequential for the categories of operators most likely to drive MVNO innovation in India: startups targeting agricultural IoT, healthcare connectivity platforms, fintech companies seeking embedded SIM services, and regional businesses serving linguistic or community-specific markets. These operators typically have the customer insight and distribution capabilities that MNOs lack, but not the capital reserves to navigate a licence structure designed for full-scale mobile operators.

For comparison, in the United Kingdom, MVNO regulation does not require separate spectrum licensing — operators simply register as communications providers and negotiate wholesale agreements with MNOs. In the European Union, the regulatory framework explicitly recognises MVNOs as competition-enhancing entities and in several member states provides for mandated wholesale access at regulated rates. India's current framework, by contrast, places the entire commercial negotiating burden on the prospective MVNO without providing regulatory backstops.

## How India Compares: MVNO Licensing Fees Across Major Markets

The most revealing dimension of India's MVNO problem becomes apparent when its licensing framework is placed alongside the regulatory approaches of markets that have successfully developed thriving MVNO ecosystems. The contrast is stark — and instructive.

### United Kingdom: No Licence Required

Perhaps the most striking comparison is with the United Kingdom, which hosts one of the world's most competitive and innovative MVNO markets — including operators such as Giffgaff, Lebara, Lycamobile, Sky Mobile, and Tesco Mobile — without requiring MVNOs to obtain any spectrum licence whatsoever.

Ofcom, the UK's communications regulator, issues licences under the Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006 to MNOs that operate physical networks. An MVNO does not require a Wireless Telegraphy Act licence as it is a customer of an MNO and is not itself a user of radio spectrum. MVNOs in the UK instead register as communications providers under a general authorisation regime — a lightweight administrative process that carries no entry fee in the conventional sense, only a proportionate annual administrative charge calculated as a small percentage of relevant turnover, applicable only once a provider's turnover exceeds defined thresholds.

In practical terms, an entrepreneur in the United Kingdom can establish an MVNO with no upfront regulatory entry fee, no net worth requirement, and no spectrum licence. The regulatory burden is proportionate, not prohibitive. The result: the UK has hundreds of registered MVNOs, serving diverse segments from ethnic communities to enterprise customers to discount-seeking consumers.

**UK Entry Fee for MVNO:** Zero (no spectrum licence required). Annual administrative charge as percentage of revenue above threshold, beginning at approximately 0.17–0.23% of relevant turnover.

### United States: Registration, Not Licensing

In the United States, MVNOs must register with the FCC and the Universal Service Administrative Company by submitting Form 499-A. Registration triggers inclusion in the FCC's Universal Service Fund system and enables agreements with major carriers. Ongoing requirements include annual 499-A filings and quarterly 499-Q revenue reports.

Crucially, this is a registration process — not a licensing process. There is no upfront entry fee for MVNO market entry in the United States beyond standard FCC administrative registration costs, which are nominal. MVNOs contribute to the Universal Service Fund based on their revenues, but this is a percentage-of-revenue ongoing obligation rather than a one-time entry barrier.

The result is a US MVNO market that has produced operators ranging from TracFone (which served approximately 20 million subscribers before its acquisition by Verizon) to Google Fi to a wide range of niche operators serving specific communities and enterprise segments.

**US Entry Fee for MVNO:** Effectively zero for market entry. FCC Form 499-A registration. USF contributions as ongoing percentage of revenues.

### European Union: General Authorisation Framework

Across EU member states, the regulatory framework governing MVNOs flows from the European Electronic Communications Code, which establishes a general authorisation regime as the baseline for market entry. Unlike India's approach — which requires a specific MVNO licence linked to the host MNO's licence — the EU framework allows MVNOs to enter markets under general authorisation without requiring spectrum-specific licensing.

BEREC required European MNOs to grant full MVNO access within 90 days of request effective 2024, cutting time-to-market for digital-first brands. This mandatory access obligation — enforced at the regulatory level — fundamentally changes the commercial negotiating dynamic for prospective MVNOs. Rather than depending entirely on the commercial goodwill of an MNO with no competitive incentive to facilitate new entrants, European MVNOs can rely on regulatory backstops to access wholesale networks at cost-oriented rates.

Individual EU member states have taken varying approaches within this framework, but the general principle is consistent: entry barriers are low, wholesale access is mandated or strongly encouraged, and the regulatory burden scales with revenues rather than being concentrated at market entry.

**EU Entry Fee for MVNO:** Nominal to zero for general authorisation. Mandatory wholesale access at regulated rates in most member states.

The data makes the policy conclusion unambiguous. Every major market that has successfully developed an MVNO ecosystem has done so by treating MVNO entry as an administrative registration exercise rather than a capital-intensive licensing process. India's current framework — with entry fees measured in crores and net worth requirements that would strain most startups — is structurally misaligned with the approaches of every comparable market.

The irony is particularly acute given that India's own telecom regulator acknowledged in its original 2008 MVNO consultation that entry barrier concerns were a central issue. Nearly two decades later, those concerns remain unaddressed while the global MVNO market has grown from a nascent concept to a USD 94 billion industry.

## What India Is Missing: The Opportunity Landscape

The cost of India's MVNO gap is not merely theoretical. Across four distinct market segments, the country is systematically foregoing economic value that a functioning MVNO ecosystem would capture.

### Agricultural IoT and Precision Farming

India's agricultural sector employs approximately 42% of the working population and contributes roughly 18% of GDP. The application of IoT to agriculture — soil moisture monitoring, crop disease detection, automated irrigation, livestock tracking, supply chain connectivity — represents one of the largest addressable markets for MVNO-enabled connectivity in the world.

Niche MVNOs with deep agricultural expertise and distribution through cooperative networks are ideally positioned to serve this market. An agricultural MVNO could negotiate bulk wholesale capacity from an MNO, deploy low-power IoT SIMs across farm equipment and sensor networks, and offer connectivity-as-a-service to farming communities at price points that the MNOs' retail structures cannot efficiently reach.

The India IoT market was valued at USD 21 billion in 2023, according to Ken Research, and is growing at approximately 19.6% CAGR through 2035. A meaningful portion of this market depends on affordable, specialised connectivity — precisely what MVNOs are structured to provide.

### Healthcare Connectivity

The expansion of remote patient monitoring, telemedicine, and connected medical devices across India — particularly in the context of the government's Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission — creates demand for secure, reliable, healthcare-grade connectivity that standard consumer mobile plans are not designed to deliver.

A healthcare MVNO could offer dedicated data channels with enhanced security protocols, service level agreements appropriate for medical applications, and patient data handling frameworks compliant with India's emerging data protection regulations. This is a segment where specialisation is commercially valuable — and where an MVNO's ability to focus exclusively on a defined vertical creates competitive advantage that no large MNO can efficiently replicate.

### Diaspora and Community Connectivity

India's diaspora of approximately 32 million people — the world's largest — generates substantial remittance flows and maintains deep communication needs with family networks in India. Community MVNOs targeting specific diaspora groups with preferential international calling rates, multilingual customer service, and culturally tailored products have built highly profitable businesses in markets with functioning MVNO frameworks.

Within India itself, the linguistic and cultural diversity of 1.4 billion people across 22 constitutionally recognised languages and hundreds of regional dialects creates natural segmentation opportunities that a national MNO serving a mass market cannot efficiently address. Regional MVNOs with local knowledge and community trust could serve these segments with a specificity that national operators structurally cannot match.

### Enterprise and Industrial IoT

As India advances its manufacturing ambitions under the Production Linked Incentive scheme and the broader Make in India initiative, enterprise connectivity requirements are becoming increasingly sophisticated. Industrial IoT deployments require guaranteed throughput, low latency, and custom network slicing capabilities that standard consumer mobile plans do not offer.

Enterprise MVNOs — which negotiate dedicated capacity from MNOs and resell it with commercial-grade SLAs to industrial customers — are a well-established model in mature markets. India's growing manufacturing sector represents a natural market for this model, particularly as the adoption of 5G-enabled industrial applications accelerates.

## The BSNL Infrastructure Argument: A Strategic Asset Waiting to Be Deployed

Perhaps the most compelling — and underappreciated — dimension of the MVNO opportunity in India is the role that Bharat Sanchar Nigam Limited could play as a wholesale host network.

BSNL is India's largest government-owned telecommunications service provider, with a subscriber base of approximately 92 million as of December 2025. Crucially, PM Narendra Modi launched BSNL's Swadeshi 4G Network in September 2025, commissioning approximately 97,500 towers developed using indigenously designed technology across the country. The network is explicitly designed to be upgradeable to 5G from a technology perspective.

BSNL's geographic footprint is its defining asset. The operator has historically maintained network presence in rural and semi-urban areas where private MNOs have limited commercial incentive to invest. This rural coverage — spanning areas where Jio, Airtel, and Vodafone Idea maintain thinner network density — represents exactly the infrastructure that rural IoT MVNOs, agricultural connectivity providers, and community operators would need to reach underserved populations.

The commercial logic is straightforward. BSNL's network infrastructure represents a substantial government investment — with total assets of approximately Rs. 167,086 crore as of 2024 — that is currently generating revenue of approximately Rs. 23,427 crore annually against operating losses. Opening this infrastructure to MVNOs as a mandated wholesale access provider would generate incremental wholesale revenue from operators who would activate demand in segments BSNL itself cannot efficiently serve, while improving overall network utilisation and accelerating the path to operational sustainability.

This is not a novel concept. In multiple markets, state-owned MNOs have found that opening their networks to MVNOs generates wholesale revenue that meaningfully contributes to financial recovery — while simultaneously advancing the government's connectivity objectives in underserved areas. BSNL's recently modernised 4G infrastructure, built on indigenous technology, is uniquely positioned to serve this wholesale function while simultaneously advancing the Atmanirbhar Bharat mission by enabling a new generation of Indian MVNO businesses.

## A Policy Reform Framework for TRAI

Based on the analysis above, a credible MVNO policy reform framework for India should address five core dimensions.

### 1. Dramatically Reduce Entry Fees

The current entry fee structure — even at the 10% of MNO rates recommended in 2008 — is disproportionate to the capital requirements and risk profile of an MVNO business. A reformed framework should establish a flat, nominal entry fee of Rs. 10 to 25 lakh for all service areas, irrespective of circle category, with the annual licence fee structured as a percentage of adjusted gross revenue rather than a fixed amount. This aligns with TRAI's broader recent recommendations on rationalising entry fees and bank guarantees across telecom licences, which noted that entry fees should stimulate competition and enhance service quality.

### 2. Establish Mandatory Wholesale Access Obligations

The absence of mandated wholesale access is a fundamental structural problem. Without regulatory backing, prospective MVNOs are entirely dependent on commercial negotiations with MNOs that have little competitive incentive to facilitate the entry of new competitors. A reformed framework should establish BSNL as a mandated wholesale access provider at regulated rates, with the option for TRAI to extend this obligation to private MNOs with market power in specific service areas if commercial negotiations consistently fail to produce viable wholesale agreements.

### 3. Create a Lightweight Licence Category for IoT MVNOs

The IoT connectivity market — which is projected to see the fastest growth of any MVNO segment globally — has distinct operational characteristics that warrant a separate, lighter regulatory treatment. An IoT MVNO licence could have lower net worth requirements, simplified reporting obligations, and a streamlined approval process, reflecting the fact that IoT connectivity services carry different risk profiles and consumer protection considerations than full consumer mobile services.

### 4. Align with the Telecommunications Act 2023 Framework

The Telecommunications Act 2023 represents an opportunity to embed MVNO licensing within a modern, technology-neutral regulatory framework. TRAI's active engagement with the DoT on service authorisation frameworks under the new Act should explicitly address MVNO-specific provisions, rather than allowing the question to remain in the administrative limbo it has occupied since 2008.

### 5. Establish a Regulatory Sandbox for MVNO Innovation

TRAI has already recommended a regulatory sandbox framework for innovative technologies and business models in the digital communications sector. Extending this sandbox specifically to MVNO applicants — allowing operators to demonstrate viability within a defined regulatory perimeter before seeking full licensing — would significantly reduce the risk for both regulators and entrepreneurs, accelerating the emergence of the MVNO ecosystem.

## The Economic Case: Why This Is a Billion-Dollar Decision

The aggregate economic opportunity from a functioning Indian MVNO ecosystem is substantial and measurable across multiple dimensions.

The Indian MVNO market was estimated at approximately USD 4.06 billion in 2024, according to Market Research Future analysis, and is projected to reach USD 6.07 billion by 2035 at a CAGR of 3.7%. Critically, this projection assumes continuation of the current constrained regulatory environment. A genuine policy reform that opens the market to new entrants — particularly in IoT, enterprise, and rural connectivity — could meaningfully accelerate this trajectory.

Beyond direct market size, the multiplier effects are significant. MVNOs that specialise in agricultural IoT would drive adoption of precision farming technologies that improve agricultural productivity and reduce input costs for tens of millions of farmers. Healthcare MVNOs would extend telemedicine access to populations currently underserved by both private providers and public health infrastructure. Enterprise MVNOs would accelerate industrial IoT adoption in manufacturing clusters that are central to India's economic growth strategy.

India's ambition to become a global IoT hub — reflected in TRAI's own recent recommendations on International M2M SIM Service Authorisation — is directly undermined by the absence of a domestic MVNO ecosystem. Building IoT connectivity expertise at scale requires a domestic market in which companies can develop, iterate, and scale MVNO-enabled IoT business models. That market does not currently exist in India in any meaningful regulatory sense.

---

## Conclusion: The Cost of Continued Inertia

The MVNO gap in India is not an obscure regulatory footnote. It is a systemic constraint on economic value creation that has persisted for nearly two decades while the global MVNO market has grown from a niche concept to a USD 94 billion industry — one in which India has barely participated.

The regulatory tools required to address this gap are well understood and have been partially articulated by TRAI itself as far back as 2008. What has been missing is the regulatory and policy will to act decisively.

The convergence of several factors makes the current moment uniquely propitious for action. BSNL's newly commissioned 4G infrastructure provides a ready wholesale platform. The Telecommunications Act 2023 provides a modern legislative framework within which MVNO authorisations can be structured. The global IoT market is at an inflection point that favours early movers. And India's ambitions — in manufacturing, in agriculture, in healthcare, in digital services — all point toward a connectivity layer that only a functioning MVNO ecosystem can efficiently provide.

Reducing MVNO entry barriers is not a concession to market disruption. It is an investment in the innovation layer that sits between India's extraordinary network infrastructure and the billion-dollar economic opportunities that infrastructure exists to enable.

The time to act is now.
